# D1 · Spatial Allocation

> This file covers: subject-share bands, negative-space grades and their emotional semantics, the textured-negative-space rule, and the quality gates for positive and negative space.
> How to use: the second thing after the aspect ratio is fixed — decide how much area the subject and the emptiness each take, and what texture the emptiness carries.

## Definition

Spatial allocation = the division of frame area between the "subject (positive space)" and the "emptiness (negative space)", together with the emotion that division carries.

## Mechanism

Negative space is not "the part left unpainted" but **deliberately designed emptiness**: it decides how isolated the subject reads, how dense the frame breathes, and it is the first source of the viewer's emotion (emptiness → solitude, crowding → tension). Negative space serves three functions at once: focus (isolating the subject), breathing (density control), and pre-reservation (leaving room for text, gaze, or the next image).

## Criteria

### Subject-share bands (rules of thumb, by subject)

| Subject | Subject share | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Face close-up / fill the frame | 70–90%+ | Boundary leaves the frame, core features complete |
| Head-and-shoulders | Face takes 60–70% of frame height | Empirical band for e-commerce headshots / ID photos |
| Half-body portrait | 45–65% | |
| Full-body portrait | 35–55% | Figure plus buffer under the feet in a vertical frame |
| Environmental portrait | 20–40% | The person becomes a point inside the environment |
| Product hero shot | 40–60% | Centered, with a grounding shadow at the bottom |
| Minimal / negative-space composition | ≤35% | Paired with a large textured emptiness |
| Landscape subject (lone tree / lone boat) | 10–30% | Scale contrast is itself the emotion |

### Three negative-space grades (subject-space ratio emotion table, consensus values)

| Grade | Semantics | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 50/50 | Confrontation, tension, equilibrium | Two-subject opposition, mirrored composition, poster positive-negative form |
| 30/70 | Comfortable breathing (the 70% emptiness version) | Off-center portrait, product poster, reserved space for cover text |
| 10/90 | Solitude, sublimity, smallness | Minimal poster, lone tree in open country, sun-disc |

> Relationship between the three grades and the five grades: the three grades are an **emotion formula** (choose semantics by subject-to-space ratio); the five grades are a **fine-tuning continuum** (adjust density by emptiness share). The same share can carry different readings — 50% emptiness sits in the sweet spot within the five grades (comfortable), yet within a 50/50 formula it reads as confrontational tension: same share, different layout (centered equilibrium versus offset confrontation), different semantics. Order of use: fix the three-grade formula by emotion first, then fine-tune density with the five-grade continuum.

### Five-grade negative-space continuum (for fine tuning, rules of thumb)

| Emptiness share | Emotional reading |
|---|---|
| 10–20% | Suffocating crowding; narrative of pressure |
| 30–40% | Compact, busy, information-dense |
| 40–50% | Sweet spot: the default starting point for most single-subject frames |
| 60–75% | Airy, quiet, grave |
| 80–90% | Isolated, sublime, nearly abstract |

Usable total range is about 30–70% (the 40–50% sweet spot is a rule of thumb, not a settled law; move up or down by the emotional target).

### Textured negative space (hard rule)

1. **"Hollow emptiness" is forbidden**: the negative-space region must be describable in texture or atmosphere — fog, paper grain, dusk gradient, water reflection, grain, a faint glow in shadow. "Flat colored background" emptiness is the source of cheapness; emptiness must be "something", not merely "nothing".
2. **The direction of emptiness must be designed**: the emptiness is larger on the side the subject faces, looks toward, or moves toward (directional negative space), so that the blank becomes a container for "what is about to happen" (isomorphic with lead room in D4/framing-room).
3. **Emptiness must not be sealed off**: leave at least one traversable visual path inside the empty region (a weak leading line / light band / gradient), or the frame becomes "subject plus vacuum".

### Negative-space dual checklist (conditions for using and avoiding — emptiness is an option, not a default)

- **Use when** (most must hold): the subject is small, isolated, solitary; scale or vastness must be conveyed; the emotion is quiet, minimal, melancholic; the background is simple and clean; the subject's silhouette is strong and readable.
- **Avoid when** (any one hit means proceed with caution): the empty region is itself cluttered; the subject lacks a clear outline; energy and tension are wanted; the background competes with the subject for attention; multiple subjects need to relate to one another.

### Quality gates (two negative constraints, for self-check)

- The empty region must not be stuffed with clutter ("no cluttered background").
- Every element in the frame must answer "why is it here" ("keep only what is necessary").

## Boundaries

- Share numbers are a design starting point, not a bullseye: emotional intent may override the band (a close-up on an oppressive theme may sit below the usual share; a lone tree on a sublime theme may go as small as 10%).
- A multi-subject frame has no single "subject share"; switch to zonal allocation (see multi-subject).
- Negative-space grade and aspect ratio interact: a vertical frame naturally carries more vertical emptiness, so count the grade by "the actual remaining area after the subject" rather than by imagination.

## Counterexamples

- Counterexample 1: subject at 30% centered with blank space split evenly all around — the isolation is canceled by the centering and the frame turns inert. Fix: offset plus directional negative space.
- Counterexample 2: a poster's empty region described only as "blue background" — emptiness without texture, and the frame reads cheap. Fix: state the texture (matte gradient / grained paper / faint water sheen).
- Counterexample 3: emptiness pushed to 85% for minimalism, while the subject's edge presses tight against the frame — it does open up, but the subject "hits the wall". Emptiness and edge margins must be designed together (see edges-cropping).
