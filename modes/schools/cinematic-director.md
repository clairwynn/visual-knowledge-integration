# Cinematic Director

## Manifesto

1. The frame is one cell of the narrative: every frame answers "where has the story got to".
2. Camera position is attitude: the height and angle of the lens are the director's stance toward the character.
3. Light is dialogue: who stands in the light and who in the dark is who is speaking.
4. Blocking is composition: the stance and facing of figures are the map of dramatic relations.
5. Even a single frame carries the frame before and the frame after: a still image holds the direction of time.

## Core thinking

Difference from painting schools: painting may compose for composition's sake, but a film frame always carries a narrative task — every composition decision must answer "what does this cell do in the story" (establish? confront? reveal? aftermath?). The unit of thought is not frame elements but **dramatic relations**: who is stronger, who looks at whom, who knows what. The power grammar of camera position (D4/angle) is this school's native language.

## Technique library

1. **Establishing frame**: EWS plus environmental depth plus time-bearing light (dawn or dusk) states where and when. Criterion: the geography is readable. Use: the first frame of a series or story.
2. **Power angle**: low angle grants authority, high angle grants vulnerability, eye level grants equality — choose by the character's current standing. See D4/angle.
3. **Blocking triangle**: three figures form a triangle, the dominant one taking the apex, the gaze flowing along the edges. See D1/multi-subject.
4. **Over-the-shoulder dialogue**: OTS frames the dialogue relation, the foreground shoulder defocused, the dominant speaker taking the larger share of the frame.
5. **Framed voyeurism**: doors, windows, gaps as inner frames — "what stands between us and the protagonist". The rhetoric shifts with the nature of the frame: shelter, confinement, or spying.
6. **Dutch-angle loss of control**: a 15–30° tilt declares "the world has gone wrong"; tilt is consistent across the whole sequence.
7. **Silhouette declaration**: backlight crushes the figure into a shape — hidden identity, solitude, ritual. Criterion: a distinctive outline.
8. **Light-zone split**: front-side light cuts the frame into a bright and a dark half, the figure straddling the boundary — inner conflict made visible.
9. **Gaze relay**: the figure looks off-frame (at the next cell's object) plus lead room — the single frame carries time direction. See D4/framing-room.
10. **Shot-size ladder narrative**: wide → close presses into emotion; close → wide pulls away in farewell. The shot-size distribution across a sequence is the emotional curve.
11. **Foreground business**: defocused foreground objects (a glass, window mullions, smoke) add spatial layers and scene texture. See D1/depth-layering.
12. **180-degree discipline (single-frame version)**: keep left-right relations continuous across series frames (A left, B right, not swapped in the next frame) — spatial geography stays consistent.
13. **Aftermath frame**: an empty shot after the event — smoke, scattered objects, an empty chair — letting what was not drawn become the subject (negative-space narrative).

## Quantitative criteria

- Camera triple complete: every frame states shot size + angle + (focal length).
- Lighting ratio: dialogue scenes 2:1–4:1; dramatic turnarounds around 8:1.
- Lead room: 20–35% on the facing side.
- Tilt: 0° in daily scenes; 15–30° for a declaration of loss of control; consistent across the sequence.
- Shot-size sequence: adjacent frames jump no more than 2 steps (empirical — too far reads as a jump cut).
- One line of narrative function per frame: if you cannot say what this cell does, the composition is beautiful and still a dead frame.

## Applicable scenes

- Storyboards, story cards, cinematic posters, narrative illustration, comic panels, single frames of a brand story film.
- Keywords: cinematic, narrative, dramatic, lens feel, atmosphere, tension.
- The cinematic recipe for a single frame: wide aspect ratio + shallow depth of field + single source + layered depth + gaze direction — all five together.

## Differences from other schools

| Compared school | Line |
|---|---|
| Classical Academy | The academy makes the frame self-sufficient; the Director makes the single frame serve the sequence — a picture to be admired alone goes to the academy, a cell that tells a story goes to the Director |
| Photographic Optics | The Director chooses camera position from dramatic need; Optics derives composition from physical property — same 85mm, the Director asks "who is humiliated", Optics asks "how much compression" |
| Documentary | The Director arranges everything; Documentary waits and arranges nothing — controlled narrative versus trusted accident |

## Counterexamples and boundaries

- Counterexample 1: every frame Dutch-tilted with backlight and shallow depth of field — stylistic overload, narrative drowned. One dramatic emphasis at a time.
- Counterexample 2: applying sequence discipline to a single image (forcing a 180-degree line) — one frame has no neighbor, so the discipline has no object. Sequence rules only operate inside a sequence.
- Counterexample 3: reducing the cinematic to a filter (adding letterbox bars and teal-orange grading) — the substance of the cinematic is the narrativity of camera position and light, not color grading.
- Boundary: this school governs the single frame and relations between frames; continuous timeline (camera movement, editing rhythm) is out of scope — camera-movement vocabulary is borrowed only as a single-frame cross-section (for example the spatial effect of a dolly zoom becomes the composition idea of "background scale contrast").
